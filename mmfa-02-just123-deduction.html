Make Math Fun Again · Post 2 of 7

Teach a child to prove it, not guess it.

Just 123 has been finished and sitting quietly in the store. This is its first proper introduction — what is inside it, and what it actually trains.

The format

What one puzzle looks like

Every puzzle in both books is built from only the digits 1, 2 and 3. No other number appears anywhere — not in the questions, not in the answers. That single constraint is what makes the books work, and it is why a calculator is no help at all.

Junior · One move

12 − 3 = 11

The equation is false. Swap any two digits — one move, nothing added, nothing erased — and make it true.

13 − 2 = 11

Trade the 2 and the 3. It is the only swap on the card that works — and that word only is the whole point of the book.

Senior · Fill in the blanks

◻³ = 1331

Each blank holds a number built from 1, 2 and 3. No calculator, and no trial and error needed.

11³ = 1331

A cube ending in 1 must have a root ending in 1, and 1331 is far too small for 21. The answer is settled before any cubing happens.

The point

What it trains: deduction

Most arithmetic practice rewards one thing — getting the number out fast. That skill has been fully automated. What has not been automated is looking at a statement and working out what must be true about it. That is deduction, and it is what these two books drill, one card at a time.

How a child actually solves it

12 − 3 = 11

Watch the reasoning, not the arithmetic.

“The right side is 11, so it cannot move — it has no other digit to trade with that keeps it small. So the fix is on the left. Twelve minus three is nine; I need eleven, so the left side has to grow by two. Swapping the 1 and the 2 gives 21 − 3, far too big. Swapping the 2 and the 3 gives 13 − 2 — eleven. Done, and nothing else could have worked.”

13 − 2 = 11

Three or four sentences of pure elimination, and one subtraction at the very end. That ratio is the design.

  • Elimination comes before arithmetic. With only three digits and one legal move, a child can hold every possibility in their head at once. The search is small enough to reason about instead of grinding through — which is exactly when reasoning starts to feel easier than guessing.
  • Uniqueness forces a reason. Each Junior card has exactly one swap that works. A child who lands it by luck still has to explain why the others fail, and that explanation is the actual lesson. “It works” becomes “it must work, and nothing else can.”
  • Structure beats speed. The Senior puzzles are settled by shape — last digits, size, whether a number can possibly be a square or a cube — long before anything is computed. This is the first taste of the idea that you can know an answer without calculating it.
  • It transfers. Ruling out three wrong options on a test, finding the one line that broke the program, working out who could not have done it in a story — same move. Deduction is one skill wearing different clothes, and this is a low-stakes place to practise it.
